How much should a baby sleep? Sleep by age and safe sleep tips

Updated October 1, 2026 · 3 min read

Newborns sleep about 14–17 hours in 24 hours, in short stretches. From 4 to 12 months most babies need 12–16 hours including naps, and toddlers aged 1–2 need 11–14 hours. Always put your baby to sleep on their back, on a firm, flat surface with no pillows, bumpers or soft toys, in the same room as you for at least the first 6 months.

Baby sleep is famously unpredictable. These ranges come from paediatric sleep recommendations; your baby may sleep a little more or less and still be perfectly healthy.

Sleep needs by age

AgeTotal sleep in 24 hTypical naps
0–3 months14–17 hoursmany, irregular
4–12 months12–16 hours (including naps)2–4
1–2 years11–14 hours (including naps)1–2

Newborns sleep in stretches of 2–4 hours around the clock because they need to feed often. A day–night rhythm usually starts to appear at around 6–12 weeks.

Wake windows

“Wake windows” are how long a baby can comfortably stay awake between sleeps. They are a rough guide, not a rule:

  • 0–2 months: 45–90 minutes
  • 3–5 months: 1.5–2.5 hours
  • 6–9 months: 2–3.5 hours
  • 10–18 months: 3–5 hours

Signs of tiredness — staring, yawning, rubbing eyes, fussing — are often more useful than the clock.

Safe sleep: the essentials

These steps reduce the risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) and accidents:

  • Back to sleep for every sleep, day and night.
  • A firm, flat, waterproof mattress in a cot, crib, bassinet or Moses basket that meets safety standards.
  • A clear sleep space: no pillows, quilts, bumpers, positioners or soft toys.
  • Room sharing — keep your baby’s crib in your room for at least the first 6 months.
  • Smoke-free before and after birth.
  • Do not overheat: light layers or a baby sleeping bag, and keep the head uncovered. A room temperature of about 16–20°C (61–68°F) is comfortable for most babies.
  • Avoid sleeping with your baby on a sofa or armchair, and never share a bed if you or your partner smoke, have drunk alcohol, or have taken drugs or medicines that make you drowsy.
  • Inclined sleepers, baby nests and car seats are not designed for regular sleep — move your baby to a flat surface.
  • Stop swaddling as soon as your baby shows signs of rolling.

Breastfeeding and offering a dummy (pacifier) at sleep times are both linked with a lower risk of SIDS.

The 4-month sleep regression

Between about 3 and 5 months, many babies who were sleeping well start waking more often. Their sleep cycles are maturing, so they come into light sleep more often and notice if something has changed — for example, if they fell asleep feeding and woke up in the crib.

It helps to keep a calm routine, put your baby down drowsy but awake sometimes, and be patient. It usually passes within a few weeks.

A simple bedtime routine

From about 6–8 weeks you can start a short, predictable routine: a bath or wash, clean diaper, pyjamas, a feed, a song or story, then lights out. Keep night feeds quiet and dim.

When to talk to a doctor

Ask for advice if your baby snores loudly, pauses in breathing during sleep, seems unusually sleepy or hard to wake, or if lack of sleep is affecting your own wellbeing. Asking for help is a sign of good parenting, not failure.

Logging sleep for a week or two often reveals a pattern that is hard to see when you are tired.

Frequently asked questions

When do babies sleep through the night?＋

It varies a lot. Many babies can sleep a 6–8 hour stretch by 6–9 months, but night waking is normal well into the second year, especially during illness, teething or developmental leaps.

What is the 4-month sleep regression?＋

Around 3–5 months, sleep cycles mature and become more like an adult’s, so babies wake more easily between cycles. It usually settles within 2–6 weeks.

Is it safe for my baby to sleep on their tummy?＋

No — always place your baby on their back to sleep. Once your baby can roll both ways on their own, you do not need to turn them back, but still start every sleep on their back.

Medical disclaimer

This guide is general information, not medical advice. Every baby is different. If you are worried, contact your doctor, midwife or health visitor. Seek urgent help if your baby has trouble breathing, is unusually sleepy or floppy, or is under 3 months old with a temperature of 38°C (100.4°F) or higher.
