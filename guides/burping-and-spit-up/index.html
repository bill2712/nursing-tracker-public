Burping and spit-up — how to burp a baby and when to worry

Updated October 1, 2026 · 2 min read

Burp your baby halfway through and after each feed — over your shoulder, sitting on your lap, or lying across your knees. Spitting up a little milk is very common, peaks at around 4 months and usually stops by 12 months. Smaller, more frequent feeds and keeping your baby upright for 20–30 minutes afterwards can help. Forceful or green vomit, blood, poor weight gain or signs of dehydration need prompt medical advice.

Babies swallow air while feeding, especially from a bottle or when feeding quickly. Burping helps bring it up so they feel more comfortable and can feed a little more.

Three ways to burp a baby

  1. Over the shoulder. Hold your baby upright with their chin resting on your shoulder. Support their bottom with one hand and gently pat or rub their back with the other.
  2. Sitting on your lap. Sit your baby on your knee, leaning slightly forward. Support their chest and chin with one hand (keep fingers away from the throat) and pat their back with the other.
  3. Face down across your lap. Lay your baby on their tummy across your knees, with their head slightly higher than their chest, and gently rub their back.

Keep a muslin cloth handy — some milk often comes up with the burp.

When to burp

  • Breastfed babies: when switching sides and at the end of the feed.
  • Bottle-fed babies: every 60–90 ml (2–3 oz), and at the end.
  • Any time your baby pulls away, squirms or seems uncomfortable during a feed.

Why babies spit up

The muscle at the top of a baby’s stomach is still maturing, so milk can easily flow back up. This is called reflux and it is normal. Most babies spit up at least sometimes; it usually peaks around 4 months and improves once they sit up and start solids.

How to reduce spit-up

  • Offer smaller feeds more often.
  • Try paced bottle feeding and make sure the teat flow is not too fast.
  • Burp during and after feeds.
  • Keep your baby upright for 20–30 minutes after feeding.
  • Avoid tight clothing or diapers around the tummy.
  • Do not raise the head of the crib or put your baby to sleep on their side or front — back to sleep is still safest.

When to see a doctor

Get medical advice the same day if your baby:

  • vomits forcefully (projectile), especially between 2 and 8 weeks old;
  • brings up green or yellow-green vomit, or blood;
  • has fewer wet diapers or other signs of dehydration;
  • is not gaining weight or refuses feeds;
  • seems in pain, arches their back and screams during feeds;
  • has a fever, a swollen tummy, or breathing problems.

Writing down feed times, amounts and when spit-up happens can help your doctor understand what is going on.

Frequently asked questions

What if my baby doesn’t burp?＋

Try for a minute or two and change position. Some babies, especially breastfed ones, swallow little air and do not need to burp after every feed.

How much spit-up is normal?＋

It often looks like more than it is. If your baby is gaining weight, has plenty of wet diapers and seems comfortable, regular spit-up is usually nothing to worry about.

When is spit-up reflux disease?＋

When it causes problems — poor weight gain, feeding refusal, frequent crying with feeds, breathing problems or blood in the vomit. Talk to your doctor if you notice these.

Medical disclaimer

This guide is general information, not medical advice. Every baby is different. If you are worried, contact your doctor, midwife or health visitor. Seek urgent help if your baby has trouble breathing, is unusually sleepy or floppy, or is under 3 months old with a temperature of 38°C (100.4°F) or higher.
