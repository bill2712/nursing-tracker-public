Breastfeeding is natural, but it is also a skill that you and your baby learn together. The first two to six weeks are usually the hardest. With good support, most problems can be solved.

## Getting a good latch

A deep latch protects your nipples and helps your baby get milk efficiently.

1. **Get comfortable.** Sit or lie back with your back supported. Bring your baby to you rather than leaning over.
2. **Tummy to tummy.** Hold your baby close, with their head, shoulders and body in a straight line, nose level with your nipple.
3. **Wait for a wide mouth.** Let your baby’s chin touch the breast and wait until they open wide, like a yawn.
4. **Bring baby on quickly.** Aim your nipple towards the roof of their mouth. More of the areola should be in the lower jaw than the upper.

**Signs of a good latch:** lips turned out, chin pressed into the breast, cheeks round (not dimpled), rhythmic sucking with pauses and swallowing, and no pinching pain after the first few sucks.

If it hurts, slide a clean finger into the corner of your baby’s mouth to break the suction and try again.

## How often to feed

Newborns feed **8–12 times in 24 hours**, sometimes more. Feeds are often irregular. Follow your baby’s cues rather than a strict schedule — frequent feeding is how your body learns how much milk to make.

In the first weeks, wake your baby to feed if they sleep longer than about 4 hours, unless your midwife or doctor advises otherwise.

## Cluster feeding and growth spurts

Many babies want to feed almost constantly for a few hours, often in the evening, or for a couple of days around 2–3 weeks, 6 weeks and 3 months. This is normal and does not mean you have too little milk. It is your baby increasing your supply for their next stage of growth.

## Is my baby getting enough?

- At least 6 heavy wet diapers a day from about day 5.
- Yellow stools by about day 5, usually several a day in the early weeks.
- Back to birth weight by about 2 weeks, then steady weight gain.
- You can hear or see swallowing during feeds, and your breasts feel softer afterwards.

Logging feeds and diapers in a tracker for a few days can be reassuring, and it gives your health visitor useful information.

## Looking after yourself

- Drink to thirst and keep water nearby while you feed.
- Eat regular meals; you do not need a special diet.
- Rest when you can, and accept help with everything else.
- Check with a pharmacist or doctor before taking medicines — most are compatible with breastfeeding, but some are not.

## Common problems

**Sore or cracked nipples** — usually a latch problem. Get help early. Expressed breast milk or a lanolin cream may soothe the skin.

**Engorgement** — very full, hard breasts around days 3–5. Feed often, hand-express a little to soften the breast before latching, and use cold compresses after feeds.

**Blocked duct or mastitis** — a tender lump, or a red, hot area with flu-like symptoms. Keep feeding from that side and see a doctor if you have a fever or it does not improve within 24 hours.

## When to ask for help

Ask a midwife, health visitor, breastfeeding counsellor or lactation consultant if feeding hurts, your baby seems unsatisfied after most feeds, there are fewer wet diapers than expected, or your baby is not gaining weight. Many areas also have free breastfeeding helplines and support groups.
